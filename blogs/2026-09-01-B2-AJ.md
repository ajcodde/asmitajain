# The Impracticality of a Microbe-Free Existence

The Anthropocene Syndrome–the interrelated challenges of rapid climate change, environmental and resource degradation, health inequalities, and social injustices–defines a crisis in which humans are both perpetrators and victims. Microbial life, however, occupies a striking position: it is indispensable to our survival, yet we are not indispensable to it. 

The habitability of planet Earth was established by microbial processes and remains microbially regulated. The Great Oxidation Event (~ 2.4 – 2.1 Gya), triggered by cyanobacteria evolving oxygenic photosynthesis, drove the evolution of eukaryotic life, including humans. Today, microbes catalyze essential biogeochemical processes, including nitrogen fixation, nitrification, denitrification, carbon mineralization, and phosphorus recycling, sustaining the conditions necessary for complex life. Human attempts to replicate these processes, such as the Haber-Bosch process for nitrogen fixation, come at significant environmental cost, accounting for 1.2% of global carbon emissions; microbes perform these and dozens of other cycles without penalty.

Microbes are also essential to human health. Our gut microbiome releases essential metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), vitamin K/B, and secondary bile acids, necessary for regulating host immunity, metabolism, and maintaining gut barrier integrity. Dysbiosis, or displacement of microbial communities in the gut, has been found to be associated with the increased occurrence of several chronic ailments, including cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). Germ-free mouse models further confirm this dependency, exhibiting increased tumour development relative to conventional mice. These show that even if humans replicate major biogeochemical processes, our development is tightly linked to the microbial communities that live in and on us.

Microbes have, throughout Earth’s history, not just survived but thrived in conditions that seem extreme to us. Their capacity for horizontal gene transfer and functional redundancy allows them to rebuild their communities even after significant population loss during major extinction events: cyanobacteria, for instance, have survived since the Great Oxidation Event.  If human activity renders the planet uninhabitable for complex life, microbial life will persist, a testament to their resilience and independence from us.

Human-made systems currently lack the redundancy and efficiency of microbe-derived biogeochemical processes; in fact, they are actively degrading them. While microbes are resilient organisms that can outlast the damage, complex life is not. As the only species capable of deliberately altering Earth’s systems, we bear the responsibility of protecting them not only for our own survival but for the millions of species whose existence depends on the stability we are undermining.
